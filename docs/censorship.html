First Day - THE FOURTH STORY

Censorship details for this story coming soon.

Although much of this story was left the same, there are some distinct passages that were subtly changed in order to distance the crude behavior of the Abbot from the moral righteousness of the Catholic Church. One such example of these changes are the passage in paragraphs 2 and 3 which describe the monk discovering the young girl and taking her to his bedchamber.

In Boccaccio’s original, the description is relatively neutral and only makes moral comment on the actions in an appropriately ironic tone for the subject matter: “Wherefore, accosting her, he entered into parley with her and so led on from one thing to another that he came to an accord with her and brought her to his cell, unperceived of any.” Very obviously, there is a certain disregard for the supposed moral authority of the Clergy, and the Monk is treated as an unfortunately, but not surprisingly, sinful fool.

Florio’s censored version from 1620 explicitly moralizes this passage. In a somewhat clumsy manner, a substantial preamble is made to emphasize the aberrant behavior of this particular monk: “Lascivious desire, and no religious devotion, made him draw neere her, and whether under shrift … or some other as close conference, to as pernicious and vile a purpose, I know not: but so farre he prevailed upon her frailety, and such a bargaine passed betweene them, that … he wonne her to his Chamber, before any person could perceive it.”

In another section of the story, (paragraph 6) an obvious edit is made to the description of the Abbot’s transgressions. Where Boccaccio provides a (mildly) scandalous description of sexual acts, Florio omits most of the suggestiveness and instead simply says: “ The Maide … was easily wonne to the Abbots will, which caused him to embrace and kisse her often.”

This story seems to violate numbers 7 and 8 in the Church’s rules of censorship. Parts of the work are “obscene” or “immoral”, and Florio treats these passages as “isolated heresies” that are then corrected.

Third Day - THE TENTH STORY

Censorship details for this story coming soon.

Possibly the most egregious example of censorship and editing is displayed in this story. The original seems to have been so offensive that it was not deemed salvageable by the Church’s standards, and so an entirely different story was put in its place. This new story has a very deliberate and reactionary moral overtone that seeks to undo the influence of the original.

The replacement story tells a saccharine tale of a princess so dutiful and pure that she will not look a man in the eye. This is a far cry from the original in which another young girl is manipulated into performing sexual acts with a monk who she believes is teaching her how to serve God. It is not at all surprising that this change was made, seeing as there is almost no way to redeem the clergy depicted in the original. However, one aspect of the changed version is interesting for a different reason.

While the original story depicts a young girl supposedly pursuing Christianity (even in a satirical way), the revision is set in Denmark and mentions the Norse Gods without relying on Christianity as a moral compass. Given the precarious position the Catholic Church was in during the ongoing Reformation, it is surprising that the revisions they implemented favored a “pagan” tale taken from a French Renaissance author: François de Belleforest. This implies that it was seen as more important to omit the obscene depictions of the clergy, even if the more modest story failed to promote the Church outright.

Heavily violating the 7th rule of censorship, this story was always destined to be changed if not fully removed in any Church-approved revisions.

Seventh Day - THE SECOND STORY

Censorship details for this story coming soon.

This story became a target of Catholic Church censorship predominantly because of the triumph of vice that is displayed throughout the story. The story depicts a woman cheating on her husband and successfully tricking him without facing any punishment or divine justice, directly in violation of the seventh rule of the Tridentine Index.

The first major change comes with the description of the main female character in the story, Peronella. The censored version seemingly attempts to place more of the blame for the future events of the story on Peronella, describing her in the third paragraph as a “fayre and lustie young Woman.” The corresponding text in Boccaccio’s original describes her more tastefully as, “a fair and lovesome damsel.” By describing her in this manner, Florio’s translation places the blame on Peronella’s lust rather than those same qualities found in the men of the story.

The largest changes come during the climax of the story. Boccaccio’s signature satirical style comes into play when describing the affair of Peronella and Gianello. He describes the comical happenings of the affair in an overly dramatic fashion, writing, “ on such wise as in the ample plains the unbridled stallions, afire with love, assail the mares of Parthia, he satisfied his juvenile ardour.” This dramatization of the quick final encounter of the lovers into something of Greek mythology works to make a joke out of the entire situation. Florio’s censored version forgoes almost an entire paragraph of description, choosing rather to boil the entire final encounter down to, “To be breefe, the Brewing Fat was neatly cleansed, Peronella and Striguario both well pleased, the money paide, and honest meaning Lazaro not discontented.” There are small bits of thinly veiled sexual innuendo leading up to this final line, but nothing as blatant as Boccaccio.
